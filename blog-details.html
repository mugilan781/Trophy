The Lost Art of Hand Engraving: How We Keep Tradition Alive in a Digital Age

In an era of automated laser systems, we believe the human hand still creates something no machine can replicate. Here's why we still train master engravers — and what they produce.

RT
Rajesh Trophywala
Founder & Master Engraver · 37 years experience
Share:

When I first learned engraving from my father in 1987, the only tool available was a sharp steel graver and a steady hand. There were no computers, no lasers, no digital previews. Just metal, tool and intention. Today, we have all those technologies — and we use them enthusiastically. But we have never stopped teaching hand engraving.

Why? Because in the 37 years I have spent in this craft, I have seen something remarkable: when a recipient holds a hand-engraved award and runs their finger across the letters, something happens that never occurs with laser-engraved work. They slow down. They feel it. They understand that another human being made that mark specifically for them.

The Physics of the Hand

A laser engraves by burning material at consistent, programmable depths. The result is technically perfect — uniform depth, uniform width, precisely spaced. It's beautiful work, and for many applications, it's ideal.

Hand engraving is different. The pressure, angle and speed of the graver change with each stroke. On a trophy's curved surface, the engraver compensates instinctively for the changing plane. Each letter gains a subtle variation in line weight — heavier at the start of a stroke where pressure is highest, tapering as the hand lifts. This is called shading, and it's what gives traditional engraving its three-dimensional quality.

"A machine can cut a letter. Only a human can give it character." — Rajesh Trophywala, Master Engraver

This shading quality has been prized since the Roman Empire. The inscriptions on Trajan's Column in Rome show the same technique — thick strokes and thin serifs that catch light differently at every angle, making the letters readable from 30 metres below.

What We Teach Our Apprentices

Our apprentice programme lasts three years. In the first year, students do nothing but practice cuts on scrap metal — straight lines, curved lines, spirals, crosshatching. They are not permitted to engrave a single letter until their cuts are consistent and controlled. This seems cruel to impatient 22-year-olds, but it mirrors the training method used by European guilds for centuries.

The Six Core Cuts

Every engraving tradition builds from six fundamental graver cuts. Our apprentices must master all six before advancing:

  • The Straight Line — the foundation of all Roman lettering, requiring perfectly consistent pressure
  • The Concave Curve — used for the interior of O, C, G, D and similar rounded letters
  • The Convex Curve — for exterior curves on letters and decorative flourishes
  • The Flick — a finishing stroke used to create tapering serifs and fine hairlines
  • The Crosshatch — for shading backgrounds and creating texture in relief work
  • The Bright Cut — an angled cut that creates a bright, reflective face on metal

For truly special awards — retirements, lifetime achievements, royal commendations — we always recommend a hand-engraved piece. The recipient will notice the difference immediately, and so will anyone who handles it.

Where Laser and Hand Engraving Work Best Together

We don't treat this as an either/or question. The most extraordinary awards we create combine both techniques. The laser handles large areas of consistent text — names and dates on 200 medals, for instance. Our master engravers then add hand-finished borders, decorative flourishes and any custom artwork that requires artistic sensitivity.

Crystal awards are always laser-engraved from the interior — hand engraving on crystal is technically impossible without risking fracture. But the base plaques on our premium crystal trophies are frequently hand-engraved, creating a beautiful contrast between the precision of the laser and the warmth of the hand.

Preserving the Craft for Future Generations

In 2023, we launched the T&A Engraving Apprenticeship Foundation, offering fully-funded three-year training to young craftspeople from across India. Our goal is to graduate 50 master engravers over the next decade — enough to seed studios in every major city and ensure this extraordinary skill never disappears from India's creative heritage.

If you have ever wondered whether the awards you commission are truly made with human craft — at T&A, the answer is always yes. Every piece carries the mark of human hands, human intention and 37 years of accumulated wisdom about what it means to honour someone with a physical, lasting object of beauty.

The tools have changed. The hand has not.
